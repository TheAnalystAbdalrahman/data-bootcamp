# Sources and teaching use

Reviewed for this planning draft on **21 October 2026**. Article pages may change. The lecture structure, synthetic exercise, Arabic wording, and proposed portfolio outputs are original teaching material, not reported findings from these sources.

## Supplied links

### S1 — ISO: An easy guide to understanding healthcare data analytics

[Read the article](https://www.iso.org/healthcare/data-analytics)

Use for: the four analytics categories, operational questions, and supply-chain applications. This is an introductory article on ISO's site, not a specific ISO standard or a compliance checklist.

Teaching adjustment: the article says analysis requires vast amounts of data. Do not repeat this generalization. Beginners can analyze a small, relevant table. Replace the prediction-as-crystal-ball metaphor with an uncertain estimate under assumptions.

### S2 — Park University: Data Analytics in Healthcare: Transforming Patient Care Delivery

[Read the article](https://www.park.edu/blog/data-analytics-in-healthcare-transforming-patient-care-delivery/)

Published 16 October 2024. Useful introductory framing for care, operations, and the four question types. It is an institutional blog, not a primary clinical effectiveness study.

Teaching adjustment: investigating explanations is not the same as proving causes; treatment and other high-stakes recommendations require more than a descriptive pattern.

### S3 — Northeastern Bouvé: Data Analytics in Healthcare: How to Apply Your Skills

[Read the article](https://bouve.northeastern.edu/news/data-analytics-in-healthcare/)

Published 21 May 2025. Use its discussion of operations, public health, and transferable analytical skills to broaden the opportunity picture. Its intended audience already includes people with analytics backgrounds; this lecture needs a gentler starting point.

Limit: career and programme promotion is not evidence of local hiring conditions. Do not adopt salary, shortage, or easy-entry claims without separate current research.

### S4 — KMS Technology: 8 Applications of Healthcare Data Analytics You Need to Know

[Read the article](https://kms-technology.com/blog/data-analytics-in-healthcare/)

The page displayed a publication date of 8 June 2026 at review. Use as an industry example bank, including resource allocation and operational reporting. It is a software-services company's marketing article.

**Core teaching use, reinforced after instructor feedback:** its “Different Types of Data Analytics in Healthcare” section names descriptive, predictive, prescriptive, and diagnostic analytics. The article also explicitly frames analytics as serving to reduce uncertainty. Scene 5 teaches these same four types in descriptive → diagnostic → predictive → prescriptive order, linked by that shared purpose. The uncertainty examples and Arabic explanations are our teaching adaptation. Qualify the purpose: suitable analysis can reduce uncertainty and make remaining gaps clearer; it cannot guarantee certainty.

Limit: do not build the opening around global data-volume percentages, projected savings, or unverified performance claims. Use specific questions and directly inspectable examples instead.

### S5 — Prezent: How data analytics in healthcare is shaping the future of pharma

[Read the article](https://www.prezent.ai/blog/data-analytics-in-healthcare)

The page displayed a last-updated date of 4 May 2026 and this pharma-focused title at review. Useful prompts include research data, medicine safety reporting, and communication across the product lifecycle. This is a commercial communication-platform blog.

Limit: specialized pharma work is not a beginner qualification obtained from a general bootcamp. Treat these as directions for further learning.

## Primary-source additions

### S6 — WHO: Sudan conflict and refugee crisis, report covering March–April 2025

[Read the WHO report](https://cdn.who.int/media/docs/default-source/2021-dha-docs/20250527_sudan-emergency-sitrep-10.pdf)

**Relevant location:** PDF page 3, “Situation update in Sudan.” It documents early warning reporting and incomplete or delayed submissions, alongside practical reporting barriers. This directly supports a Sudan-specific example about the importance of data availability and timeliness.

Teaching use: ask whether an absent report means no events occurred. Keep this explicitly dated. It does not establish current 2026 coverage, and it does not show that any particular beginner analysis caused a health outcome.

### S7 — WHO: Early Warning, Alert and Response System (EWARS)

[Read the WHO overview](https://www.who.int/emergencies/surveillance/early-warning-alert-and-response-system-ewars/)

WHO describes a system intended to support outbreak detection in emergencies, including difficult settings with unreliable connectivity and electricity. Use to show why collection, reporting, review, and response form a chain.

The classroom diagram is a simplified explanation. An alert requires investigation; it is not itself confirmation of an outbreak. Do not attribute every system function to a single analyst or suggest a classroom project replaces surveillance expertise.

### S8 — WHO: Health Inequality Monitor data

[Explore the data repository](https://www.who.int/data/inequality-monitor/data)

WHO provides data broken down by population characteristics, supporting comparisons that an overall figure can conceal. Use as the global counterpart to the Sudan reporting example.

Before selecting a real dataset for later coursework, check its indicator definition, source, population, period, breakdowns, and limitations. The repository notes that some external-source data are not WHO official estimates.

## Evidence boundaries for the lecture

- Medicine-availability figures are fictional and demonstrate reasoning, not the prevalence of a real problem.
- Possible work directions are a synthesis of tasks and domains, not a labour-market study.
- No salaries, placement rates, job counts, guaranteed outcomes, or claims that tools alone qualify someone for specialist work are included.
- Healthcare applications illustrate what analysis can contribute. Any actual benefit depends on suitable evidence, responsible interpretation, feasible implementation, and review.
- Patient care, public health, transport, water, finance, and education examples are connected through questions and decisions; their methods and domain requirements are not interchangeable without adaptation.
